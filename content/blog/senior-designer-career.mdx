I have been a 'senior product designer' for longer than I was a junior or mid-level designer combined. The title has stayed the same. The role has not.

What changed is not visible in a job description. It's visible in how problems are approached, what questions get asked, and what battles are worth fighting.

## The output shifts

Junior designers produce artefacts. Wireframes, prototypes, specs. The work is tangible and measurable.

Senior designers still produce artefacts, but the primary output is clarity. Clarity on what problem we're solving. Clarity on why a particular approach is right. Clarity on tradeoffs between competing constraints. The artefacts are in service of that clarity, not the end in themselves.

This is a hard transition to make because artefacts are what get praised in portfolio reviews. "Beautiful work." "Clean layouts." The clarity work is invisible until it isn't there.

## The conversation changes

A junior designer advocates for good design. A senior designer advocates for users inside the language of the business.

This sounds cynical. It isn't. It's just accurate. In any product organisation, design competes for resources and attention with engineering, business development, legal, and marketing. If you speak only in design language, you will only be heard by designers.

When I present research findings, I translate them into business risk. "Users abandon at this point" becomes "we are losing X% of potential conversions at this step, equivalent to approximately Y ARR." Same finding, different frame. Different conversations get unlocked.

## The hardest skill

The hardest skill I've developed is knowing when to push and when to concede.

Not every battle is worth fighting. Some design compromises are genuinely painful but not harmful. Some are genuinely harmful and need a champion. Distinguishing between them requires judgment that only comes from watching the consequences of past decisions play out over time.

The designers I most admire are not the ones who fight for every detail. They are the ones who know which details matter, who have a clear hierarchy of what can bend and what cannot, and who fight hard for the things that actually affect users, not their own aesthetic preferences.

## On mentoring

Senior designers grow by teaching. This sounds like advice for the junior people on the team. It isn't.

Teaching forces you to articulate things you do intuitively. Why did you structure that information architecture that way? Why is that button placement wrong? The intuition is often correct, but articulating the reasoning is how you test it, and how you discover the cases where your intuition is actually just habit.

The best feedback I ever gave to junior designers was the feedback that made me realise I didn't fully understand my own reasoning.

## The fifteen-year view

What I know now that I didn't know at five years: design is a long game. Careers are long. Industries change. The tools I used when I started don't exist anymore in the same form. The tools I use now will be different in ten years.

The durable skills are not the tools. They are: asking the right questions before starting, listening carefully to people who use what you design, making decisions from evidence rather than opinion, and communicating clearly across the full range of people who shape a product.

Those skills compound. Everything else is replaceable.
