Most apps don't lose users because the product is bad. <mark>They lose them because the path to using it is annoying.</mark>

None of the fixes below need a redesign. Most take an afternoon. All of them are easy to miss until someone points them out.

---

## Don't Ask For Permission Before It's Needed

Notifications, location, camera, contacts — apps love asking for these the moment someone opens them for the first time. Most people say no on reflex, because there's no context yet for why it matters.

Wait until the exact moment the permission is actually useful, and explain in one line what they get for saying yes:

- Ask for **camera access** when someone taps "scan," not on the welcome screen
- Ask for **notifications** after someone creates something worth being notified about
- Ask for **location** when it changes what's on screen, not before

> The same request, asked at the right time, gets said yes to far more often.

## Show Errors Right Away

A generic "please fix the errors below" banner makes people hunt across the whole form to find what went wrong. Some just give up.

**Put the message right under the field**, the moment the mistake happens, in plain language:

- Not "invalid input" — "that email's missing an @"
- Not "field required" — "we need your name to create your account"

Small change. Fewer abandoned forms.

## Build Trust At The Paywall

Right before someone pays, they've mostly stopped thinking about features and started thinking about risk. *Will this actually work? Can I get my money back if it doesn't?*

That's the moment to answer those questions directly:

- A short, real review
- A clear refund policy
- A visible guarantee

<mark>Trust signals earlier on the page do less work than the same signals shown at the exact moment someone's about to commit money.</mark>

## Make Loading Feel Fast

A blank screen with a spinner starts to feel broken after about a second, even if the app is working fine.

A skeleton layout that shows the shape of the content, or a progress bar that visibly moves, feels alive instead. The actual wait time doesn't change — what changes is whether the person waiting believes something is happening.

> That belief is often the difference between someone staying on the screen and someone tapping back.

## Let People Pick Up Where They Left Off

If someone closes the app mid-signup, mid-checkout, or mid-onboarding, don't send them back to the start when they return. Save their progress and drop them right back where they stopped.

Starting over feels like punishment for stepping away, even when the interruption wasn't their fault — a phone call, a notification, a train arriving. <mark>A flow that remembers where someone was gets finished far more often than one that resets on them.</mark>

## Shorten Signup

Ask for an email and a password. That's the whole first step.

Job title, company size, how they heard about you, phone number — none of that needs to happen before someone's even inside the product. **Every field on that first screen is a chance to lose someone who was one tap away from being a user.**

Move the extra questions to later, once they've seen enough value to want to answer them.

## Simplify Onboarding

New users want to see the product work, not read about how it works. A long tour, a checklist of setup tasks, a series of "here's how this feature works" screens — most of that gets skipped or forgotten anyway.

Find the shortest real path to the first moment someone thinks *"oh, that's useful,"* and cut everything standing between them and that moment.

> Explaining can happen later, once they already care.

## Guess What You Can

If you already have a reasonable guess at someone's timezone, currency, or plan size based on team headcount — fill it in rather than asking. Let them correct it if it's wrong. That's a much smaller ask than typing it from scratch.

<mark>Every field you can pre-fill accurately is one less decision standing between someone and finishing the task they came to do.</mark>

## One Clear CTA

When a screen has three buttons fighting for attention, most people either freeze or click the wrong one.

Every screen should have exactly **one obvious next step** — visually bigger, higher contrast, impossible to miss. Secondary actions, alternate paths, "maybe later" links can still exist, but they should look like what they are: not the main event.
