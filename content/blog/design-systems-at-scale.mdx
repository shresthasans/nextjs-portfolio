Every design system starts with the same optimism. A Figma file. A Notion doc. A kickoff meeting where everyone agrees this time it will be different.

Most design systems die quietly. Not with a bang but with a gradual drift: components that fall out of sync with production, a token system nobody uses, documentation that's six months stale, and eventually designers going rogue because it's faster.

After building three systems from scratch across very different organisations, I've developed some hard opinions.

## The first mistake: starting with components

It feels natural to start with buttons. Buttons are tangible. You can show them to stakeholders. You can tick them off a list.

But buttons are downstream of decisions you haven't made yet. What is your spacing scale? What does "primary" mean in your system: is it your brand colour, or is it contextual? What's your border radius philosophy? If you can't answer these, your button component will be wrong in ways you can't see yet.

**Start with tokens, not components.**

Tokens are your system's vocabulary. Define colour (semantic aliases, not hex values), spacing (a scale with a clear rationale), typography (a limited set of text styles, not a font picker), and elevation. Then build components that consume those tokens.

When your tokens are right, changing your brand colour is a one-variable change. When they're wrong, it's 400 find-and-replace operations.

## The second mistake: building for perfection

The first version of every design system I've built has shipped incomplete. This used to bother me.

It doesn't anymore, because I've learned that an incomplete system that's actually used beats a perfect system that's too heavy to adopt. Ship the 20 components that solve 80% of the use cases. Ship real documentation even if it's rough. Ship something your first consumer team can build with _today_.

Adoption is the only metric that matters. A design system with no users is just an art project.

## What nobody talks about: governance

The hardest problem in design system work is not the design. It's the governance.

Who decides when a component should go in the system versus stay in a product-specific library? Who reviews pull requests? What happens when Product Team A wants to modify a component in a way that breaks Product Team B's usage?

You need a decision-making framework _before_ you have the first conflict, not after. The conflict will come. Teams will want exceptions. Engineers will have strong opinions about implementation. Designers will diverge.

My recommendation: a small system team (1–2 designers, 1 engineer) with explicit decision authority, a lightweight RFC process for additions, and a versioning strategy that lets product teams stay on older versions while migrating at their own pace.

## On documentation

Documentation is a product. It has users (your colleagues), it has jobs to do (answer questions before they become Slack messages), and it degrades if you don't maintain it.

The best documentation I've written has been written _while building the component_, not after. The context is freshest then. You know why you made the decisions you made. Six months later you'll have forgotten.

Two things in every component doc: **usage guidelines** (when to use this, when not to) and **accessibility requirements** (keyboard behaviour, ARIA patterns, focus management). Everything else is useful. Those two are essential.

## The long game

A design system is a living product. It is never done. The systems that succeed are the ones that have someone who cares about them: someone who treats component requests as product requirements, advocates for the system's value in roadmap meetings, and maintains documentation the way a library maintains its catalogue.

If nobody owns the design system, nobody will use it.

That's not an abstraction. It's the reason most design systems die.
