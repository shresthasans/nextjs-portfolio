## Overview

I redesigned LinkedIn's homepage feed on my own, without a client brief, a team, or access to LinkedIn's internal data. I picked a page every reviewer already uses, so they can judge my reasoning against something real instead of taking my word for it.

The feed wasn't broken in one obvious way. It was broken in fourteen small, specific ways that all traced back to the same root cause: nothing on the page had a rule for what deserved visual weight, so a puzzle widget, an ad, and the content a user actually came for competed on equal footing. <mark>Auditing against real accessibility criteria showed that almost every hierarchy problem and every accessibility problem were the same failure wearing different clothes.</mark> I rebuilt the page around one consistent rule for what gets attention and why.

- **Scope:** Solo, self-initiated — no client brief, no LinkedIn internal data or engineering constraints, 5 peers for interviews and testing
- **Process:** [Heuristic evaluation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-conduct-a-heuristic-evaluation/) → [contextual inquiry](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-inquiry/) → [WCAG](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/) accessibility audit → [information architecture](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/information-architecture/) → [interaction design](https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/topics/interaction-design) & visual design → [usability testing](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/)
- **Finding:** 14 documented issues, all tracing back to one root cause
- **Fix:** One consistent rule for what earns visual weight, applied module by module
- **Validation:** Moderated before/after usability test with the same 5 peers — task success rate, time-on-task, and a post-task ease rating, not just my own judgment
- **Framing:** An audit trail and defensible judgment, not a validated production outcome

## Context

LinkedIn's homepage feed does a lot on one screen: composer, feed, navigation, network suggestions, trending topics, profile stats, entertainment widgets, and ads, all at once. That density isn't a mistake — every module earns its place for a real business reason like engagement, retention, or ad revenue.

<mark>I picked this page because that tension is real and unavoidable.</mark> Deleting everything that isn't the feed isn't judgment, it's scissors. I wanted to see how much clarity I could recover without pretending the commercial pressures behind the clutter don't exist.

## The Problem

Nothing on the existing feed was catastrophically broken. It was death by a thousand small ambiguities, clustered into three kinds of failure.

- **Nothing signaled what mattered most,** a [visual hierarchy](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-hierarchy-ux-definition/) breakdown. Composer, feed, profile card, sidebar widgets, and ad units all carried roughly the same visual weight. A puzzle-game tile used the same saturated color treatment as a person's name. Users either tuned out most of the page or scanned past it out of fatigue.
- **Identity, monetization, and vanity metrics were blended into one object.** The profile card stacked a user's name and photo, a "Redeem Premium for $0" upsell, and stats like profile views, separated only by hairlines, so a user could mistake the ad for part of their own identity. The cover photo even had the user's name baked into the image — redundant with the heading below it and invisible to a screen reader.
- **Basic engagement information had no accessible name.** Reactions showed up as a cluster of colorful icons and a bare number, no text label anywhere. That's a real accessibility gap: a screen reader has nothing to announce for "24 people liked this."

<mark>Left alone, the page would keep asking every user, sighted or not, to do the work of figuring out what mattered, every time they opened it.</mark>

## Initial Understanding

Going in, my assumption was that this was a visual polish problem — the page "felt busy" and needed calmer styling. What I didn't know yet was how much of that busyness was one page doing two structurally different jobs at once (platform navigation and page navigation, crammed into a single row), and how much was accessibility debt that had nothing to do with color.

My constraint was self-imposed: no internal data, no LinkedIn user base to test against, only 5 peers willing to give me their time. So I held myself to an honest heuristic and accessibility audit, backed by a small peer test, rather than claiming validation at a scale I don't have.

## Discovery & Learning

Before the audit, I ran a [think-aloud](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/thinking-aloud-the-1-usability-tool/) [contextual inquiry](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-inquiry/) with 5 peers, regular LinkedIn users, none of them designers, watching them narrate their own feed with no script beyond "show me what you check first, and tell me anything that confuses you."

Two patterns showed up across almost everyone:

- **Quick scrollers** (3 of 5) skimmed past the right rail entirely and couldn't recall what was in their own profile card afterward.
- **Careful readers** (2 of 5) noticed the premium upsell, but two of them assumed it was tied to their own account status, not an ad.

People either ignored the sidebar or misread it as their own data. That's what pointed the audit at the profile card and the right rail specifically, instead of treating every module as equally suspect.

With that steer, I ran a [heuristic evaluation](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-to-conduct-a-heuristic-evaluation/) paired with a formal [WCAG 2.1](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG21/) accessibility audit, module by module: [color contrast](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) (text and icons need enough contrast to be readable, not just present), [landmark and heading structure](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/info-and-relationships.html) (sections need real markup a screen reader can navigate, not styling that looks like structure), [consistent labeling](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/consistent-identification.html) (the same action needs the same label everywhere), and [focus order](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/keyboard.html) (every element reachable and visibly focused with a keyboard alone).

I expected a list of unrelated small bugs. Instead, <mark>almost every accessibility failure stood in the exact same spot as a hierarchy failure.</mark> The nav's active-tab indicator, a thin underline only, failed a legibility bar *and* left users with no fast way to tell where they were, one failure showing up twice. The reaction icons had no [accessible name](https://www.w3.org/TR/accname-1.2/) (the text a screen reader announces for an element), and were also the least scannable part of the page for sighted users, since meaning depended on tiny glyphs instead of a word. The profile card's baked-in cover text was invisible to a screen reader *and* a visual redundancy for sighted users, since the real name already appeared as a heading below it.

That pattern held across all fourteen issues I catalogued, from small polish fixes through moderate ones (buried post headers, no post length limit, ambiguous reshares) to structural ones (a three-column layout with no shared grid, a nav row doing two jobs). None were isolated. They were one problem, described fourteen ways.

## Defining the Real Problem

That was the turning point. I'd started this framed as "clean up the visual noise." After the audit, I reframed it as <mark>"give the page one consistent rule for what earns visual weight, using accessibility criteria as the mechanism for deciding that, not a checklist applied after the layout is done."</mark>

In practice, contrast ratios, touch target sizes, and landmark structure weren't things I checked once the redesign was finished. They were the tool I used to decide, module by module, what deserved to be louder and what needed to get out of the way.

## Exploring Solutions

A few directions came out of that reframe, and I didn't treat every option as equally worth exploring.

**The overloaded nav row** (an information architecture problem) crammed platform utilities (search, notifications, profile) and page destinations (Home, Jobs, Messaging) into one line at equal weight. The lighter fix — raising icon contrast in place — wouldn't have fixed the real problem: two different questions competing for one visual treatment.

**The profile card** (an IA and content-strategy problem) blended identity, an ad, and vanity stats behind only a hairline. A stronger divider between the ad and the identity content still leaves an ad living inside a "this is you" component, and doesn't touch the accessibility issue baked into the cover image.

**The original three-column layout** (profile rail, feed, widget column, a layout grid and [responsive design](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/responsive-web-design-definition/) problem) could have just kept its three columns and gotten tighter spacing between them. Once the profile card was gone, though, that left rail had no real reason to stay independent, and tighter spacing alone wouldn't stop the right-side widgets disappearing on scroll, so the redesign collapses down to two columns instead.

**Reactions and other engagement actions** (an interaction design and accessibility problem) could have kept their icon-only look with better color contrast between icons. That satisfies a contrast checker but gives a screen reader nothing to announce, which was the actual gap.

## Design Decisions

These four decisions carried the most weight in the redesign.

### 1. Splitting one overloaded nav row into two, by function

*Discipline: Information Architecture, Interaction Design*

**The challenge:** Eight-plus items doing two different jobs sat in a single row at equal weight. Users had to scan the whole bar to figure out where they were.

**Alternatives considered:** Keep one row and raise icon contrast, which fixes legibility but not the real problem: two different questions competing for one visual space.

**Why I chose this:** Platform utilities ("what can I do anywhere") and page navigation ("where am I") are genuinely different jobs. A blue utility row and a white destination row let each do exactly one thing, and it's what made a legible active state possible — Home now gets bold blue text plus the underline, not just a hairline nobody notices.

> **Expected impact:** Faster orientation, since users no longer parse eight equally-weighted icons to find their place.

### 2. Removing the profile identity/ad/stats card from the feed view entirely

*Discipline: Information Architecture, Accessibility (WCAG)*

**The challenge:** Name, photo, and title sat stacked with a "Redeem Premium for $0" upsell and stats like profile views, separated only by a hairline. The cover photo had the user's own name burned into it — redundant for sighted users and invisible to a screen reader.

**Alternatives considered:** Keep the card, add a stronger divider between identity and ad. That still puts monetization content inside an identity component and leaves the image accessibility issue untouched.

**Why I chose this:** Identity, advertising, and vanity metrics are three different jobs. The honest fix wasn't better labeling, it was refusing to let them share one object. Removing the card also removes the alt-text problem at its source.

> **Expected impact:** Eliminates the accessibility failure entirely, and frees vertical space for content relevant to the user's network.

### 3. Collapsing three independent columns into one two-column system, with a persistent right rail

*Discipline: Information Architecture, Visual & Layout Design*

**The challenge:** The profile rail, feed, and widget column ran as three separate modules with no shared grid, reading as bolted-together pieces. Right-rail content also vanished the moment a user scrolled.

**Alternatives considered:** Keep three columns, just tighten the grid. Once the profile card was gone, that column had no reason to stay independent, and tighter spacing alone wouldn't stop content disappearing on scroll.

**Why I chose this:** With identity content removed, the feed could take the full width it needs, and a sticky rail keeps trending topics and suggestions accessible without scrolling back up.

> **Expected impact:** One cohesive layout instead of three independent modules, with secondary content reachable at any scroll depth.

### 4. Giving every engagement action a visible text label instead of icon-only reactions

*Discipline: Interaction Design, Accessibility (WCAG)*

**The challenge:** Likes, comments, shares, and saves showed up only as colorful icons plus a bare number. No accessible name existed anywhere, and meaning depended on recognizing small icon shapes.

**Alternatives considered:** Keep icon-only, raise color contrast. That satisfies a contrast checker but gives a screen reader nothing to announce.

**Why I chose this:** The clearest case in the audit where the accessibility fix and the usability fix were the same fix. Adding the word next to the icon — "Like," "Comment," "Share," "Save" — and spelling counts out ("24 reactions · 8 comments") solves both at once.

> **Expected impact:** Every action and count is legible to a screen reader and a fast human scan at the same time.

## Iteration

A handful of moderate fixes fell out naturally once the four decisions above were in place. With the nav split and the profile card gone, there was room to compress post headers from up to four stacked metadata lines down to two: name and degree on one line, title and timestamp in gray below. A "Show more" length cap, a [progressive disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/) pattern, followed the same logic: an unbounded post would have undone the rest of the newly scannable feed. Reshared or quoted posts got their own nested, bordered card, a [Gestalt proximity](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/) cue, so the "X likes this" wrapper reads as a distinct group instead of blending into the original post's header.

Two fixes stayed smaller, and I'm flagging their limits rather than overclaiming. Renaming "Sort by: Top" to "Showing: Relevant" improved the control's [information scent](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-scent/) (how clearly its label signals what it actually does), but it still doesn't carry the visual weight it deserves as the only control that changes what content a user sees. Replacing "Today's puzzles" with "Trending" hashtags, and adding a one-line reason to every network suggestion ("Works at your company," "In your industry"), were simpler swaps: entertainment widgets and unexplained suggestions don't deserve the same real estate as content tied to a user's professional context.

## Validating the Redesign

Once the redesign was built, I brought back the same 5 peers and ran a moderated, within-subjects [usability test](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-testing-101/): same four tasks, once on the current live LinkedIn feed, once on the redesign, order randomized per person to control for learning effects.

| Task | Metric | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Time to identify current nav section | [Time-on-task](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/success-rate-the-simplest-usability-metric/) | avg 6.4s | avg 2.1s |
| Correctly stated what a reaction icon + count meant, unprompted | [Task success rate](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/success-rate-the-simplest-usability-metric/) | 2 of 5 | 5 of 5 |
| Mistook the profile card's ad for their own account data | Error rate | 3 of 5 | 0 of 5 |
| Found trending topics again after scrolling down the feed | Task success rate | 0 of 5 (all scrolled back up) | 5 of 5 (used the sticky rail) |
| "Easy to tell what matters on this page" (1–5) | [Single Ease Question](https://measuringu.com/seq10/) | avg 2.4 | avg 4.2 |

One peer who'd assumed the premium card was "part of my profile" in the first session opened the redesign and said, unprompted, "oh, that's clearly an ad now." Another, previously one of the quick scrollers who missed the right rail entirely, found the trending topics without hesitation and asked why the old version didn't just do this.

<mark>Five people is a signal, not a sample.</mark> I'm treating this as directional support for the four decisions, not proof the redesign works at LinkedIn's actual scale and diversity of users.

## Outcome

This project didn't ship and was never going to. It's a self-directed audit, not a production initiative, so what I have is a small, real before/after test on top of the audit, not production telemetry.

<mark>What I can stand behind is the audit trail and the test behind it:</mark> fourteen specific, documented issues checked against real WCAG criteria, decisions that trace directly back to those findings, and a before/after test that measured something instead of assuming it, not LinkedIn's own analytics, but a real, small, directional result. The redesigned feed keeps every module the original had a legitimate business reason to include, ads, upsells, suggestions, but restructures which of them get to compete for attention and why.

## Reflection

This project confirmed that accessibility and visual hierarchy are usually the same problem wearing two names. The invisible nav state that failed a contrast check was also why users couldn't orient quickly; the icon-only reaction cluster with no accessible name was also the least scannable part of the page for anyone. <mark>Fixing one without the other means doing the work twice.</mark>

With a larger, more diverse group of real LinkedIn users, I'd want to test whether removing the profile card and its stats is right for everyone, since all 5 peers in my test skewed toward the "quick scroller" or "careful reader" patterns I saw in the interviews. A wider sample might surface a pattern I didn't catch.

> Almost every accessibility failure on this page was standing in the exact same spot as a hierarchy failure. Fix one without the other and you end up doing the work twice.

## Key Takeaways

- **This pass touched every core UX discipline, not just visual design:** heuristic evaluation, contextual inquiry, information architecture, interaction design, accessibility engineering, and usability testing all fed the same four decisions.
- **Fourteen separate-looking issues traced back to one root cause.** The page had no consistent rule for what earned visual weight, so ads, games, and real content all competed equally.
- **Accessibility criteria worked as a design tool, not a compliance checklist.** Auditing against specific WCAG success criteria is what surfaced that hierarchy and accessibility problems were almost always the same failure.
- **Structural honesty sometimes means deleting a component, not restyling it.** The profile card's identity/ad/stats ambiguity wasn't solvable with a better divider; it needed to come out of this view entirely.
- **The clearest wins were the ones that fixed two problems with one change,** like adding text labels to reactions, which solved an accessibility gap and a scannability gap simultaneously.
- **A 5-person before/after test turned "expected impact" into a measured one,** even at a small scale: faster nav orientation, zero remaining ad/identity confusion, full task success on the sticky rail.
- **Not every fix in this pass is fully resolved,** and I've tried to flag the ones that aren't (like the sort control's visual weight) rather than present the whole audit as a closed case.
